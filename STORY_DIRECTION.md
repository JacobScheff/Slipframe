# Slipframe — environment-led story direction

A reconstruction computer is rebuilding incomplete places from stored observations. It can reproduce their appearance more accurately than their physical causes. The player helps it check movement, clearances, combinations and timing by traveling through those reconstructions.

The world carries the mystery: storms have speakers and fans; crystals emerge from precise sockets; scenery has detailed fronts and supported backs. Repeated natural arrangements reveal manufactured regularity. These clues reward attention without requiring the player to stop or read text.

The computer may be an AI, but a clear purpose matters more than the label. It could keep rebuilding a meaningful place because it cannot accept the limits of the surviving record. This remains a narrative proposal, not established game canon.

## Level roles

- Ember: convincing surfaces with small construction faults.
- Summit: elevation tests and engineered footholds within mountain scenery.
- Low Crawl: service infrastructure and clearance fixtures.
- Ghost Glass: incomplete geometry, transparent surfaces and fractured panes.
- Storm: weather reproduced through machinery.
- Crystal: validation of compatible fragments. Different-color merging is an analogy, not a literal binary algorithm.
- Lattice Forge: validated fragments assembled into usable geometry.
- Gyre: timing alignment through rings, shutters and encoder machinery.

## Approved visual direction

Remove the humanoid phantom, projected hands, wrist symbols and character-shaped scans. Each level should feel cohesive. Use its existing material family for detail and communicate through construction, rather than conspicuous signage.

Gyre uses plum and violet metal, muted lilac channels, pale fasteners and lavender opening edges. Lattice Forge uses its turquoise machinery palette and a fabrication station.

## Possible resolution — not implemented

The computer learns to leave an uncertain region unfinished instead of endlessly inventing a continuation. A quiet junction could let the player acknowledge the missing fragment. The archive remains accessible afterward for ordinary replay.
